---
title: What developers should get good at in the agentic era
date: 2026-11-27
description: What building ShipperKit with agents taught me about system design, taste, and getting people to actually use what you make.
---

I've been programming since I was 12, and what hooked me was the flow of it. That moment when something finally clicks and the thing in your head actually runs. So I get it when developers say they're scared of agents taking that away. I really do.

What they don't realize is that the joy doesn't disappear, it moves up a level. Zoom out a bit, start designing in bigger components, and you'll find the exact same click in orchestrating a system that you used to find in writing a function. We already got really good at the small components. Code is basically a solved playground now. The bigger blocks are where the fun is.

So here's what I'd tell a developer who asked me what to get better at right now.

The first thing is system design, and specifically learning how to delegate to agents without taking yourself out of the loop. You want to be on top of the loop. Close enough to everything that what you're doing is really hard to replace with just another agent. Delegate just enough that you trust what comes back, and grow it from there. This should be gradual, not an overnight switch where you hand everything off on Monday. You're building up a model in your head of how to delegate tasks and whole loops, and that only comes with reps.

Second, stop putting your tech stack in your bio. I said it on X and I'll say it again: if you still have your tech stack in your bio, you're NGMI. A list of frameworks doesn't say much anymore. What you should be able to show off is a project people use. Emphasis on use. There are just no excuses left to not have at least one project with 100 active users. Getting there means you coordinated a lot of moving parts and touched a bit of business and marketing along the way. It's proof that you can be responsible for a system, and that's basically the job now.

The part of engineering that got way more important for me since working with agents is finding new problems to solve. It takes a completely different mindset. We've massively increased our capacity to build, but demand hasn't caught up, because our minds are so used to the status quo that we still can't fathom what we're able to build now. I built ShipperKit because I didn't like Coolify and thought, we can do this simpler and better. Five or six years ago, a solo dev saying that would have sounded crazy.

And I built it (still building it) with agents. I even gave it a VPS as a playground so it could test things out on the real internet. But I was actively in the loop the whole time, because human taste for flows and interfaces is still nowhere to be found in AI. I might be wrong here, but I think taste is so complex and so abstract that it can't be encoded easily. Parts of it can, sure. But even we can't really define it. So I made the architectural choices based on what my goal was, and I filled in the gaps wherever the flow was off, connecting the whole user experience together.

One trick that helps a lot with the taste problem: whenever I do any kind of design with AI, I just tell it "make 15 variations." AI tends to regress to the mean. Ask it for one landing page and you'll get something basic with a few issues. Then you start prompting to make it better, and something still feels off, and the offness is so big it's not worth prompting out. Ask for 15 instead and there's a much bigger chance one of them is close to what you had in your head, so you can start iterating from there. Take it one step further and ask for variations per section, then pick and choose like building blocks. This works in a lot of fields, mostly interfaces and anything visual. Where it falls apart is backend work. There's usually no use for 15 approaches there. The AI will find one or two that are considered optimal, and it'll pick the one that also benefits the developer.

When an agent writes most of a change, the thing I still want to understand is the flow of it. I'm very product oriented, which I think is one of the most important things to be in this era, and I like to think of software and features as products. They're ready. They're wired in properly. Picture a room with electrical cables hanging out of the wall, some trash in the corner, the paint still wet and the bucket on the floor. Software can look exactly like that. What I want is all the loops properly closed, maximal clarity, no dead ends, no gaps. That final polish you can feel when you use apps from big companies. That's the part I want to understand as deeply as possible, with my own hands, before I ship.

If I were learning to code today, I'd deliberately practice bare bones scripting. High school level stuff, like analyzing data from files in Python, or writing Skript for a Minecraft server. Something close to human language but still code enough to teach you the concepts, because that's the prerequisite for jumping to the next abstraction level, which is AI. The rest I'd happily learn with an agent from day one, and it'll feel really intuitive. Things like classes and polymorphism were always code problems, not logic problems. AI basically solved coding, so those can be abstracted away. What we need to understand are the logic concepts, and how to scale them beyond code to whole systems. Code is just a really good playground to learn them in.

Once you can build faster, the time you save should go into everything that isn't building. For me that's definitely marketing. Learning how to sell (I'm still very early on this path), talking to people, getting inspired so your taste keeps improving, and trying angles there simply wasn't enough manpower for ten years ago. It also means getting into multitasking, so you're not bottlenecking yourself by running and babysitting a single agent thread. And honestly, download the mobile app for whatever agent you're using and go touch some grass. Your agents can keep running while you're away from your desk.

So where's the line between shipping now and taking another week? If the product has any uncertainties, or it's missing basic features that day 0 users would definitely reach for, or it just feels rough and unpolished, I keep going. Those are all vibe based and you can't really measure them, but you can feel them. Feature-wise, I'll give it another week if there's a more optimal way to do something: research how, and maybe do some hill climbing with AI to optimize a specific stat like speed or file size. But that week has a purpose. It's not an excuse to chisel the same app for the 200th time this month. If what's left is polish for polish's sake, go launch it. Upload some TikToks, send those cold emails, write those DMs. Everything is possible now, and you'll fix stuff later.

The take I keep seeing and actually want to argue with is that AI will take all office and engineering jobs. Yes, it will take the current form of them. But these jobs look completely different from how they looked 20 years ago, and they'll look completely different 20 years from now. People see that we can jump an abstraction level and hand the lowest layer of our work to a tool, and then they stop there. They don't think about the fact that jobs, positions and whole industries constantly evolve, and that keeping up with that change is part of the job. If you don't, you won't be employable in that form. AI is taking junior positions right now because the definition of junior hasn't changed yet. It will. Junior refers to someone starting out in an industry, not to a specific skill set or way of working, and what it takes to be a junior in five years will look very different.

So if you want to get better at building with agents, here's what to do this week. Think of a program, website or mobile app you always wanted to build but never had the skills or the time for. Download an agent to your laptop and tell it to make it for you. You'll run into every single thing I've talked about here. And slowly you'll realize why the human is still needed in the loop. I'd say more than ever.

The 12 year old in me who loved that click is still here. The blocks just got bigger.
